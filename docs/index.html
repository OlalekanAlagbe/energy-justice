A data essay

Who is counted, who is powered, who is mined

Three questions about the world's energy, asked of one dataset. The answers were not the ones I expected when I started.

By Olalekan Joshua Alagbe Data Our World in Data, energy Release 2026-09-10 Rows 19,494

01Who is counted

The dataset cannot see most of the world

This dataset describes the energy of 74 countries in detail. For the other 146 it reports a total and nothing more.

A country is counted here if the dataset says how much coal, oil, gas, nuclear, hydro, solar and wind it uses. Seventy four countries get that. The rest get a single number for total energy and nothing underneath it.

The countries that are measured in detail are the rich and the large ones. In Africa, four countries out of fifty four are measured: Algeria, Egypt, Morocco and South Africa. Nigeria is not one of them. Nigeria has 232 million people, produces more oil than any other African country, and has 57 of the dataset's 66 energy columns completely empty.

This is not a complaint about Our World in Data. They publish what their sources publish, and they say so plainly in the codebook. The gap starts further upstream, in which governments and agencies collect fuel-level statistics and report them.

The gap is not closing. In 1970 the dataset could break down the energy mix of 40 countries. In 2024 it could do 74. Over the same years the count with a published total went from 65 to 218, and electricity went from almost nothing to 196 countries.

So measurement did expand, a lot, in two of the three lines. The detailed energy panel is the one that barely moved. Fifty four years, 34 more countries.

That matters for every chapter after this one. When a chart on this site shows an energy mix, it is showing those 74 countries. When it shows electricity, it can show almost everybody. I use electricity wherever a chapter needs to be about the whole world, and I say so each time.

02Who is powered

The gap has not closed in 24 years

Since 2000 the average person in a low-income country gained +16 kWh of electricity a year. The average person in a high-income country gained +575.

The world average was 3,666 kWh per person in 2023. The median country used 2,560. Nigeria used 169.4, which puts it at the 11.3th percentile of the countries that report this at all.

Neither this map nor any column in this dataset says how many households have a connection. A national average can look adequate while most people have nothing. That is a real limit and I come back to it in the last chapter.

Twenty four years of growth, and the ratio moved from 44.2x to 43.6x. Falling at that rate, it reaches parity in about 1,500 years.

The ratio is the kind view. Measured in kilowatt-hours rather than as a multiple, the gap did not narrow at all. It widened, by 558 kWh per person, to 8,963. A ratio can improve while the distance between two groups grows, and here it did.

Both lines rose. That is worth saying plainly, because the chart is not showing anything getting worse. Low-income countries generated more electricity per person in 2024 than in 2000. They just did not gain on anybody.

I cannot tell you why from this dataset. It has no data on grid investment, finance, debt, population growth by country within these groups, or policy. It can show the shape of the gap and nothing about its causes.

03The mix is not the story

How clean says nothing about how much

Across 158 countries there is no relationship between how much electricity people have and how clean it is. The correlation is 0.0002.

Shares on this page are shares of electricity generated, not of total energy supply. Our World in Data changed how it measures primary energy on 10 September 2026, from the substitution method to total energy supply. Shares of electricity are not affected by that change. Shares of total energy, used further down this page, are.

The spread in how much electricity people have is enormous. From the 10th to the 90th percentile it is a factor of 59x. The spread in how clean it is runs from 2.7% to 94.0%. Both vary hugely. They just do not vary together.

Look at the left of the chart, where countries have the least electricity. Ethiopia gets 124 kWh per person and 0.0% of it from fossil fuels. Uganda gets 110 kWh and 2.9%. Niger gets 84 kWh and 97.9%. Bangladesh gets 648 kWh and 98.3%. Nigeria gets 169 kWh and 77.3%.

Those five countries are all near the bottom for electricity and have almost nothing in common in how they make it. Grouping countries by where they sit in an energy transition does not describe this picture. Grouping them by how much they have does.

Coal and gas added 35,796 TWh of supply between 2000 and 2024. Solar and wind added 4,649. Coal and gas added 7.7x more energy than solar and wind did.

Renewables are growing fast. They are also growing on top of a fossil system that is still growing, rather than in place of it. Both things are in the same data.

04Who is mined

Producing energy is not the same as having it

Thirteen countries, 742 million people between them, dig up far more fossil energy than they use and their people get very little electricity. Nigeria is 31.3% of that group on its own.

Norway is the reason this chapter is careful. It produces 7.64x its own energy supply, more than almost anywhere, and its people use 24,866 kWh of electricity each. Qatar is in the same position. Selling energy abroad and having plenty at home are perfectly compatible.

The bottom right of the chart is where that stops being true. Chad produces 10.1x its own supply and its people get 18 kWh each. Nigeria produces 1,395 TWh against a domestic supply of 515 TWh, and its people get 152 kWh each.

What this dataset cannot tell you is why. It has nothing on contracts, ownership, refining capacity, domestic pricing or who the revenue goes to. It can show that the gap exists and how large it is. The explanation is somewhere else.

05Nigeria

One extra kilowatt-hour a year

Nigeria generates 2.82x the electricity it did in 2000. Its population grew 1.88x. What is left over is about 1.21 extra kilowatt-hours per person per year.

Both panels are true at once. The top one is a real achievement: Nigeria nearly tripled its electricity generation in twenty five years. The bottom one is what that felt like to a Nigerian household, because the population grew almost as fast.

Per person, output has moved between 116.6 and 187.0 kWh for the whole period. The world average in 2025 was 3,860. At 1.21 kWh a year, Nigeria reaches today's world average in about 3,050 years.

That last number is arithmetic, not a forecast. Rates change. It is there to give the gap a scale, not to predict anything.

Nigeria's electricity is gas and water. Gas was 61.74% of generation in 2000 and 68.68% in 2025, peaking above 83% in 2014. Hydro is most of the rest. Solar reached 0.31%.

Nigeria has no energy mix in this dataset, only an electricity mix. So this chart covers the electricity sector and not the fuel Nigerians actually burn at home. Firewood and charcoal are outside these sources entirely, and private generators do not appear as generation. The real household picture is larger and dirtier than this.

06What this cannot tell us

The limits are part of the finding

Chapter 1 was about what this dataset cannot see. So is this one. The difference is that here I am counting what the whole project could not answer, including the things I wanted it to.

Everything below was measured, not assumed. The claims that a column does not exist come from scanning all 130 column names in the frozen file. If the dataset ever gains one, this page stops saying it is missing.

Nigeria has a total and almost nothing else

I said in Chapter 1 that Nigeria is not one of the 74 countries with an energy mix. Here is what that means exactly. The dataset has 66 columns describing energy supply. 57 of them are empty for Nigeria in every year, from 1900 to 2025.

Of the 9 that are not empty, four are nuclear and read 0.00 in every year. The other five are the total and figures derived from it. So the dataset knows roughly how much energy Nigeria uses and nothing at all about what it is made of. That is why Chapter 5 is about electricity only.

Columns that do not exist

Four things a reader would reasonably want from a page about energy justice, which this dataset has no column for at all.

What is missingWhy it matters here
Electricity access No column reports the share of people with a connection. Chapters 2 and 3 use demand per person, which is a national average. A country can look adequate on average and leave most households with nothing.
Installed capacity Generation only, so nothing on how much plant exists or how much of it runs. For a country with chronic outages that is the interesting number.
Prices, tariffs, affordability Nothing on what energy costs or what share of income it takes. Energy poverty is as much about price as quantity.
Fuel imports and exports Only electricity trade is reported. Chapter 4's horizontal axis is production divided by domestic supply, which is an accounting residual, not measured trade.

What the sources leave out

The Energy Institute covers commercially traded fuels. Firewood and charcoal are outside that. For Nigeria they are most household cooking energy, so the dataset understates Nigerian energy use and cannot see the bottom of the ladder at all.

Private generators do not appear as electricity generation. Some of the fuel may sit inside the oil supply figure, but it cannot be separated. Gas that is flared is excluded from production entirely, which matters for Chapter 4.

Every figure on this site is a national average. There is no data inside countries: no urban and rural split, no income deciles, no households. National averages are exactly where energy injustice hides, and this dataset cannot go there.

The codebook gives electricity a range of 1900 to 2025. At country level that is misleading: before 1985 only the United Kingdom and the world aggregate have it. Every country comparison on this site starts in 2000 for that reason.

The methodology change, in full

Energy here is total energy supply, not the substitution method

On 10 September 2026 Our World in Data changed how it measures primary energy, from the substitution method to total energy supply, following its sources. The two are not directly comparable, and OWID say so plainly.

Under the substitution method, electricity from hydro, wind and solar was scaled up to the fossil fuel that would have been needed to produce it. Under total energy supply it is counted at the energy content of its gross electrical output. The practical effect is that renewable shares in this release are lower than the numbers most readers have seen in older charts, in news coverage, or in earlier coursework. If a figure here looks too small against something you remember, this is probably why.

There is a second problem inside the same change, and it is not visible in the column names. Non-fossil combustible sources are counted on their heat input, and non-combustible renewables on their electrical output. I tested what that does. For the world in 2024, nuclear energy supply divided by nuclear electricity generation is 3.07x. For wind the same ratio is 1.0x, and hydro and solar are 1.01.

So nuclear is counted at the heat that went in and renewables at the electricity that came out. Any figure for the low-carbon share of total energy supply inflates nuclear roughly threefold against renewables. This site avoids that figure. Where a low-carbon share appears, it is a share of electricity, where the problem does not arise.

Two things I found and did not use

Poor countries look cleaner, but only on average. In 2023 the low-income aggregate got 61.9% of its electricity from low-carbon sources against 45.7% for high-income countries. That looks like a striking reversal and it would have made a good chapter. It does not survive at country level: the median country in the lowest-generation fifth is only 37.5% low-carbon. The aggregate is pulled up by a few large hydro countries, mostly Ethiopia and the Democratic Republic of Congo. The honest version is that some poor countries run on hydro, not that poverty produces clean grids, so I left it out.

Some countries went backwards, for opposite reasons. Between 2000 and 2024, 19 of 93 countries over 10 million people generated less electricity per person than they had. Canada lost 3,523 kWh per person and the United Kingdom 2,275, which is efficiency and the loss of heavy industry. South Africa lost 600 and Venezuela 656, which is a grid in trouble. The number moves the same way and means opposite things. Charted together it would mislead, so there is no chart.

What would answer these questions

None of this is a criticism of Our World in Data. They publish what their sources publish and document the gaps carefully. The limits start further upstream, in which governments and agencies collect fuel-level statistics and report them.

The questions this site raises and cannot answer need different data: household surveys for access and affordability, utility records for reliability, and national energy balances for the countries that do not currently produce them. Those exist for some countries. The pattern of which ones is, more or less, Chapter 1.
